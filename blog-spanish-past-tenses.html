Preterite vs Imperfect: The Ultimate Spanish Past Tense Choice

SG
Sofia Gomez
Senior Castilian Philologist & Madrid Academy Dean
March 24, 2026 9 Min Read Spanish Grammar

In Spanish, talking about the past requires navigating one of the most famous grammar choices in Romance philology: choosing between the Pretérito Indefinido (Preterite) and the Pretérito Imperfecto (Imperfect). Unlike English, which often relies on a single simple past tense form ("I went", "I ran"), Spanish forces speakers to choose a tense based on the nature of the action's duration and limits.

Struggling to select the correct past conjugation is the single most common source of frustration for beginner and intermediate Spanish students. By learning the fundamental rules of **Completed Actions vs Background Habitual States**, you can automate your Castilian past-tense controls with absolute precision.

The Preterite: Pinpointing Completed Actions

The **Preterite** tense acts like a single stamp in a passport. It denotes actions that took place at a specific, completed point in time, with a clearly defined beginning and end. If the action is finished, or if you can state exactly how many times it occurred, the Preterite is your mandatory selection.

"Think of the Preterite as the plot-driving events of a movie, and the Imperfect as the background scenery, weather, and mood that frame those events."

Sofia Gomez, Senior Castilian Philologist

The Imperfect: Framing the Scene

The **Imperfect** tense acts like a video recording rather than a photo. It represents ongoing habits, background descriptions, age, time of day, weather, and emotional states in the past. It answers the question: "What used to happen?" or "What was going on when something else interrupted?"

Preterite Completed Stamp
Ayer comí paella en Madrid.
Translation: Yesterday I ate paella in Madrid.

Since 'ayer' (yesterday) defines a clear, closed temporal frame and the act of eating is a completed event, the Preterite 'comí' is grammatically correct.

Imperfect Habitual Frame
De niño, comía paella cada domingo.
Translation: As a child, I used to eat paella every Sunday.

This describes a recurring childhood habit rather than a single event. Therefore, the Imperfect 'comía' must be utilized to convey the repetitive background nature of the action.

Fluentra Pro-Tip: The "ALTEZZA" Method for Imperfect

Memorize the **ALTEZZA** trigger acronym for the Imperfect: **A**ge (tenía 5 años), **L**ocation/Status (estaba en casa), **T**ime (eran las dos), **E**motion (quería salir), **Z**ealous habits (used to/solía), and **A**tmospheric weather (hacía sol).

The Interruption Formula

In narrative Spanish, the Preterite and Imperfect constantly collaborate. We use the **Imperfect** to set the scene (what was happening) and the **Preterite** to mark the sudden action that interrupted it. For example: Yo leía (Imperfect) cuando mi hermano entró (Preterite). (I was reading when my brother entered.)

Spanish Past Tenses Key Takeaways

  • Identify limits: Clear beginning and end signals require the Preterite (e.g. *fui*, *hablé*).
  • Habitual acts = Imperfect: Recurrent habits in the past always take the Imperfect (e.g. *iba*, *hablaba*).
  • Trigger keywords rule: Keywords like *ayer* or *el año pasado* trigger Preterite; *siempre* or *cada día* trigger Imperfect.
  • Visual narrative logic: Set the movie background with Imperfect, and record the action sequences with Preterite.
